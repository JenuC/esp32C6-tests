Chapter 8

The two bytes that are not a CRC

The spec says every frame ends with a two-byte checksum, and frame[0] counts it. That is true on the air and false in the receive buffer. This chapter proves it from live traffic rather than asking you to believe it.

Sketch sketches/ch08_rssi_lqi/ch08_rssi_lqi.ino

The wrong model

The obvious reading of a 14-byte PSDU is 12 bytes of MAC frame plus a 2-byte Frame Check Sequence. Most people write their parser that way, treat the last two bytes as a checksum they do not need, and move on.

The hardware has already verified the CRC before your callback runs — that is what "receive done" means — and it reuses those two bytes to report its own measurements: RSSI, then a link-quality value.

The experiment

Do not take that on faith. The sketch proves it on-device.

The argument is simple enough to state in one line: a CRC is a function of the frame content, so identical content cannot produce two different checksums. One counterexample settles it. So the sketch remembers the leading bytes of every frame it sees, and watches for the same body arriving with different trailing bytes.

Repeats are common, which is what makes this practical. 802.15.4 retransmits when an ACK is lost, so the same bytes genuinely go out twice. Point it at a busy channel and you normally get a hit within a minute:

  **** PROOF: identical frame body, different trailing bytes ****
  body: 61 88 69 01 0B 00 00 02 00 B6 00 14
    trailer BA 06   -> rssi -70 dBm, lqi 6
    trailer BB 09   -> rssi -69 dBm, lqi 9
  A CRC is a function of the frame content, so one body
  cannot have two checksums. These are receiver metadata.

Across the 12,800 captured frames in this repository, 315 distinct frame bodies appear with more than one trailing pair, one of them with five.

Confirming what they are

Reading the first trailing byte as a signed 8-bit integer gives numbers that are unmistakably dBm, and they track physical distance exactly as they should. From the captures, grouped by channel:

A 30 dB spread that lines up with the floor plan is not what a checksum looks like. The second byte stays in a narrow range (1–11 in these captures) and behaves like a quality or correlation count.

What to actually do

Read them from frame_info. It has been the callback's second argument since chapter 3:

queue[q_head].rssi = frame_info ? frame_info->rssi : 0;
queue[q_head].lqi  = frame_info ? frame_info->lqi  : 0;

This is the documented route to the same numbers, and it does not depend on a buffer-layout detail that could change. The sketch prints both readings side by side so you can see they agree:

len 14  frame_info: rssi  -63 dBm lqi  11   tail bytes: C1 0B  ( -63 dBm,  11)
The consequence that matters

Your payload is frame[0] - 2 bytes long, not frame[0]. Getting this wrong by two bytes is how a protocol with a 3-byte payload appears to have a 5-byte one, with two trailing fields that never quite make sense and change for no reason you can find. That mistake can burn a lot of time in chapter 10.

Transmit is not symmetric

On the way out, the FCS is real. esp_ieee802154_transmit() appends a genuine CRC over your frame, and you must leave room for it in buf[0] without writing it yourself. The asymmetry is only on the receive side, and chapter 9 depends on it.

A transferable habit

The spec described the protocol correctly; it said nothing about what a particular receiver leaves in memory afterwards. When a document and your bytes disagree, the bytes are describing your actual system. Find the test that distinguishes the two readings — here, grouping frames by content — and run it.
