Chapter 10

Decoding an unknown payload

Everything so far reads parts of a frame that a standard defines. This chapter is about the part nobody wrote down — and the case study is real, with the captures in this repository so you can check the work.

Sketch sketches/ch10_decode_case/ch10_decode_case.ino

The subject: Digi XB24 radios wired to laser-safety monitors in a building, beaconing status to an indicator box about once a second. Around 12,800 captured frames. After chapter 7 the frame splits cleanly and only one part is a mystery:

61 88 46 01 0B 00 00 01 00MAC header — known
83 00 0Bpayload — ?
C1 0Brssi / lqi

Three bytes. Here is how they fell.

Step 1 — Count before you read

Before interpreting a single byte, find the fields that never change. Constants are structure; variables are content. Grouping by length and by source address:

  • Only two frame lengths existed, 14 and 5, in near-equal numbers. Equal counts of two sizes is the signature of acknowledged unicast, so the 5-byte frames were ACKs and there was really only one format to crack.
  • The destination was 0x0000 in all 6,426 data frames.
  • The middle payload byte was 0x00 in all 6,426.
  • Exactly three source addresses existed: 0x0001, 0x0002, 0x0003.

That is most of the structure, and none of it required a guess. Two unknown bytes remain.

Step 2 — Change the world and capture again

This is the step that actually decodes a protocol, and it beats any amount of passive staring. Three captures under known physical conditions, tabulating the last payload byte by source:

Capturesrc 0x0001src 0x0002src 0x0003
monitors off0A (10)14 (20)1E (30)
monitors on, laser off0A (10)14 (20)1E (30)
monitors on, laser on0B (11)14 (20)1E (30)

One byte moved, on one device, in the one capture where that device's laser was on:

payload[2] = source_address × 10 + (laser_on ? 1 : 0)

So 10/11 for monitor 1, 20/21 for monitor 2, 30/31 for monitor 3. The values read as decimal in hex — 0x0A is 10, 0x14 is 20 — which is a tell that a person chose them rather than a bit-packing scheme.

Twelve thousand passive frames could not have produced that table. One capture with a known change did it in a single pass.

Step 3 — What is left over is usually a counter

The first payload byte changed every frame. Comparing consecutive frames from the same source:

Channel / sourceFramesBoth advanced by 1Advanced together
ch 16 src 182370496.0%
ch 21 src 154749696.3%
ch 21 src 21,59883996.4%
ch 25 src 186281096.2%

It advances in step with the MAC sequence number about 95% of the time, from a different starting value, with the offset slipping by one every few hundred frames. That is a second counter maintained one layer above the MAC — Digi's own — not anything derived from the sequence number.

Because the offset drifts it cannot be computed, so a re-implementation just keeps its own counter. Which is what the real radios are doing.

The remaining 5% were exact duplicate frames: MAC retransmissions after a lost ACK. Those same duplicates are what proved the RSSI point in chapter 8 — one observation, two uses.

Doing this to your own device

The sketch runs the method as well as the result. It prints the decoded protocol in English:

monitor 1: laser ON    (pan 0x0B01 src 0x0001 seq 0x46 rssi -63 dBm)
monitor 2: laser off   (pan 0x0B01 src 0x0002 seq 0x88 rssi -62 dBm)

and every fifteen seconds it prints the tool from step 1 and 2 — a tally, per source, of every value each payload byte has ever taken:

================ payload profile ================
  src 0x0001  412 frames, 3-byte payload
    payload[0]: varies (>8 distinct values) - likely a counter
    payload[1]: constant 0x00
    payload[2]: 0x0A(10) 0x0B(11)   <-- state?
  src 0x0002  389 frames, 3-byte payload
    payload[0]: varies (>8 distinct values) - likely a counter
    payload[1]: constant 0x00
    payload[2]: constant 0x14
=================================================

Point it at something of your own, let it settle, then change something physical — press a button, open a door, switch a light — and watch which column moves. That is the entire technique.

Lock the result in with a test

A decode that lives only in a sketch rots. Copy two frames verbatim out of the capture, write the builder, and assert it reproduces them byte for byte. laser_xbee_esp/test/test_frame.c in this repository does exactly that, and runs on a laptop with no hardware:

gcc -Imain test/test_frame.c main/xbee_frame.c -o t && ./t

The on-air confirmation is the one that counts, though. Transmit your reconstructed frame at the real device and see whether it acknowledges. An ACK means its MAC accepted every field in your header — which is chapter 9, and is how this project ended up replacing an XBee with an ESP32-C6.

Write down what you guessed

Monitors 2 and 3 never showed their "laser on" codes (21, 31) in any capture. Those are inferred from the pattern, not observed. Keeping the observed and the inferred parts of a decode separate is worth the small effort — the inferences are where the next bug will be.

The method, in short

  1. Count before you read. Constants are structure, variables are content. This is free and it narrows the problem enormously.
  2. Change the world and capture again. One A/B capture under known conditions is worth any amount of passive data.
  3. Apply the standard first. Everything down to the payload boundary is already defined. Guessing offsets without reading the FCF wastes time.
  4. Test your assumptions against the bytes. Chapter 8's CRC question looked settled by the spec, and one grouping disproved it.
  5. Make the conclusion executable. A replay test against captured frames stops a later refactor from quietly breaking the format.
