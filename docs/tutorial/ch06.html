Chapter 6

Scanning the band

Stop guessing which channel to listen on. Sweep all sixteen, tally what arrives, and learn what a scanner structurally cannot see.

Sketch sketches/ch06_scanner/ch06_scanner.ino

Why hopping is necessary

Sitting on one channel finds nothing if you picked the wrong one, and from the console that is indistinguishable from "there is no 802.15.4 traffic anywhere near me". Those two situations need completely different responses, so the ambiguity has to go.

The loop is the chapter 5 sketch plus a channel change:

current_channel = next;
esp_ieee802154_set_channel(next);
esp_ieee802154_receive();   // arm once, here

Arming once per hop is the point. Calling receive() on a timer inside the dwell loop is a common habit and it is pointless — with set_rx_when_idle(true) the radio returns to RX by itself after every frame. The earlier sketches in this repository called it every 50 ms and gained nothing from it.

The duty cycle, honestly

Sixteen channels at 2 seconds each is a 32-second sweep, so any one channel is being watched 6.25% of the time.

What you are looking forDoes a scanner find it
A device beaconing on a timer (1 Hz, say) Yes. Two seconds of dwell catches one or two heartbeats every sweep.
Which channels have networks on them Yes. This is what a scanner is for.
A one-off event — a button press, an alarm, a command No. You will miss it 15 times out of 16.
The exact timing between two frames No. Hops insert gaps that are not in the traffic.

So the scanner is a survey tool. Once it has told you where the traffic is, go back to chapter 5 and pin the radio to that channel for anything that needs real coverage.

Stamping the channel at receive time

The callback records which channel each frame arrived on:

queue[q_head].channel = current_channel;

This is why a frame is always labelled correctly even when it is printed just after a hop. Its position in the output can be misleading — a frame queued moments before the hop gets printed below the next channel's banner — but the channel number on the frame itself is the one it was received on. Worth knowing before you chase a phantom cross-channel leak.

Reading the output

---- frames per channel so far ----
  ch 14 (0x0E, 2420 MHz): 56
  ch 16 (0x10, 2430 MHz): 54
  ch 17 (0x11, 2435 MHz): 55
  ch 18 (0x12, 2440 MHz): 36
  ch 21 (0x15, 2455 MHz): 119
  ch 25 (0x19, 2475 MHz): 66
-----------------------------------

That is a real sweep from this project: six channels busy, ten silent. A channel that lights up consistently on every sweep has a network on it. A single frame on one channel in one sweep is usually a neighbour's device, or noise that happened to pass CRC.

Channel numbers in hex

The summary prints both, because device documentation and configuration tools disagree about which to use. Digi's XBee tools write the channel in hex, so CH = 15 in an XBee profile means channel 0x15 = 21 decimal, not channel 15. This tutorial's repository contains a sketch named esp32-c6-listen-HEX15 that listens on channel 16 while its comments claim 15, which is what that confusion looks like in practice.

What a quiet channel does not prove

Wi-Fi occupies the same band and is far louder. A channel that reads as empty here may simply be sitting underneath an access point, with 802.15.4 frames being lost to interference rather than absent. If a device is supposed to be on a channel and the scanner cannot see it, moving the board closer is a cheaper test than assuming the device is off.
